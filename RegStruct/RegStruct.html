1 Introduction

Up to this point, we have focused on describing linguistic data: looking at distributions, calculating measures of centre and spread, and visualizing patterns across groups. Descriptive statistics tell us what our sample looks like.

The next step is to ask whether the patterns we observe are likely to reflect a meaningful relationship between variables, or whether they could plausibly arise through ordinary variation in the data.

This module introduces the basic structure of regression. We will begin with probability and hypothesis testing, move through correlation and the t-test, and then build a simple linear regression by hand before asking R to do the same calculation for us.

The goal is not to memorize formulas. The goal is to understand what a regression model is actually doing.

2 Probability and Statistical Uncertainty

Statistical analysis is fundamentally about uncertainty.

When we observe a pattern in a sample, we want to know whether it is stronger than we would expect from chance alone.

A simple coin-flipping example helps illustrate this idea.

If a fair coin is flipped once, the probability of heads is:

1 / 2
# 0.5 (50%)

If it is flipped twice, the probability of getting two heads in a row is:

(1 / 2) * (1 / 2)
# 0.25 (25%)

For three heads in a row:

(1 / 2)^3
# 0.125 (12.5%)

For five heads in a row:

(1 / 2)^5
# 0.03125 (3.1%)

As a particular sequence becomes more extreme or unusual under the chance model, its probability becomes smaller.

2.1 Hypotheses

In statistical testing, we usually distinguish between two competing ideas.

The research hypothesis represents the relationship or difference we are interested in.

The null hypothesis represents a model in which that relationship or difference is absent.

For example, suppose we want to know whether speech rate differs between two political groupings.

A possible research hypothesis is:

Speech rate differs between the two political groupings.

The corresponding null hypothesis is:

Speech rate does not differ between the two political groupings.

Statistical testing does not prove that either statement is absolutely true. Instead, it asks how compatible our observed data are with the null model. For example, if Left- and Right-leaning MPs have different average speech rates in our sample, we want to know whether that difference is large enough that it would be unlikely to appear just by chance if the two groups did not really differ.

2.2 What is a p-value?

A p-value tells us how likely we would be to see a result like ours if there were actually no real effect. In other words, if the null hypothesis and the statistical model were true.

Another way to think about a p-value is in terms of repeated sampling. Imagine that we repeatedly collected new samples using the same procedure from a population in which the null hypothesis was actually true. The p-value tells us how often we would expect to obtain a result at least as extreme as the one we observed simply through sampling variation.

A small p-value therefore indicates that the observed pattern would be relatively unusual under the null model. For example, if Left- and Right-leaning MPs differ in speech rate and the p-value is very small, that suggests a difference of that size would be unusual if the two political groups did not actually differ in speech rate.

A conventional significance threshold is:

alpha <- 0.05

Results below this threshold are often described as statistically significant. In other words, the smaller the p-value, the more evidence we have against the null hypothesis, and the more reason we have to reject it in favour of an alternative hypothesis, which may correspond to our original research hypothesis.

Importantly, a p-value is not:

  • the probability that the null hypothesis is true;
  • the probability that our research hypothesis is true; or
  • the probability that we have made a mistake.

It is a probability calculated under the null model (the model built to test the null-hypothesis).

A note on significance testing: The use of p-values, and especially the conventional \(\alpha=.05\) threshold, has been widely debated. The .05 cutoff is ultimately a convention rather than a natural boundary between meaningful and meaningless results, and many statisticians argue that effect sizes, uncertainty, replication, and the broader evidence should receive more emphasis. Despite these criticisms, p-values and the .05 significance threshold remain the standard approach in much of empirical research and are still widely expected in published work.

2.3 Type I and Type II Errors

No statistical decision is completely certain.

A Type I error occurs when we reject a true null hypothesis. In practical terms, we conclude that an effect or relationship exists when it does not.

For example, We conclude that Left- and Right-leaning MPs differ in speech rate when, in reality, there is no underlying difference between the groups. In other words, we detect an effect that is not really there.

A Type II error occurs when we fail to reject a false null hypothesis. In practical terms, a real effect exists, but our analysis fails to detect it.

Type II error: We conclude that there is no difference in speech rate between Left- and Right-leaning MPs when, in reality, the groups do differ. In other words, a real effect exists, but our analysis fails to detect it.

Sample size matters (I’ll repeat this again sample size matters) because larger datasets generally provide more information about the underlying pattern. Very small samples can produce unstable estimates and may fail to detect real effects.

3 Correlation and Relationships Between Variables

A correlation describes the relationship between two variables.

For example, we might ask whether longer words tend to have longer durations.

A positive relationship would mean that, as one variable increases, the other tends to increase as well.

A negative relationship would mean that, as one variable increases, the other tends to decrease.


A: The larger a word, the longer it is.
B: Speech rate increases, as word duration decreases.

3.1 Correlation is not causation

A relationship between two variables does not automatically mean that one causes the other.

Two variables may be related because:

  • one affects the other (what we are hoping to find!)
  • the causal direction is reversed;
  • both are influenced by a third variable; or
  • the apparent relationship arises through sampling variation or chance.
Possibility Linguistic example
One affects the other Speech rate > vowel duration. As speakers increase their speech rate, vowels tend to become shorter. Here there is a plausible causal mechanism: speaking faster leaves less time for individual segments.
The causal direction is reversed L2 use < L2 proficiency. We might observe that people who use their L2 more often are more proficient and conclude that greater use causes proficiency. But part of the relationship may run the other way: people who are already more proficient may be more willing and able to use the L2 frequently.
Both are influenced by a third variable Vocabulary size <-> lexical-decision reaction time, with age as a third variable. Older participants may have larger vocabularies but slower reaction times. Vocabulary size and reaction time could therefore appear related even if vocabulary size itself is not causing slower responses.
The apparent relationship arises by chance Speaker age <-> VOT in a very small sample. Suppose we measure only eight speakers and the older speakers happen, by chance, to have longer VOTs. The sample could show a convincing correlation even though age and VOT are unrelated in the wider population.



This is one reason regression is useful: we can examine the relationship between a dependent variable and one predictor while also accounting for other predictors that may matter.

4 From Correlation to Regression

A correlation tells us whether two variables move together.

A regression goes further by describing the expected value of one variable as a function of another.

4.2 Building a Linear Regression Model

Before looking at the equation for a linear regression, we are going to build one from a very small dataset. This will let us see where each part of the model actually comes from.

Above, we have measured the duration of eight words. For each word, we have two pieces of information:

  • the number of syllables in the word; and
  • the duration of the word in milliseconds.

Our question is:

Can we use the number of syllables in a word to predict how long the word will last?

Our hypothesis is: the greater number of syllables in a word, the longer the word’s duration will be.
Our null-hypothesis: The number of syllables in a word has no relationship with the word’s duration.

In statistical terminology:

  • Duration is our dependent variable, or outcome.
  • Syllables is our independent variable, or predictor.

We can call these \(Y\) and \(X\):

  • \(Y\) = Duration
  • \(X\) = Syllables

These are not numbers invented by the statistical model. They are the two variables that we actually measured.

duration=c(280,390,510,495,545,260,405,520) #Y
syllables=c(1,2,3,3,3,1,2,3) #X

4.2.1 Step 1: Look at the data

We just did this.

There is a fairly obvious pattern: words with more syllables tend to have longer durations.

But we want to describe that relationship more precisely.

One way to do this is to find a straight line that fits the observations as closely as possible.

Before we can draw that line, however, we need to know two things:

  1. How steep should the line be?
  2. Where should the line cross the Y-axis?

The first is called the slope. The second is called the intercept.

4.3 Step 2: Find the centre of the data

We will start by calculating the average value of each variable.

The mean number of syllables is:

mean(duration)  
mean(syllables)

\[ \bar{Y} = 425.625 \]

\[ \bar{X} = 2.25 \]

The bar over the letter (\(\bar{Y}\), \(\bar{X}\)) signifies the mean of the values.

So the average word in our dataset has 2.25 syllables.
And the average word duration in our dataset is 425.624 milliseconds.

We therefore have a point representing the centre of our data:

\[ (\bar{X},\bar{Y})=(2.25,425.625) \]

The regression line will pass through this point.

4.4 Step 3: Calculate the slope

The first thing we need to determine is how much duration changes as the number of syllables changes.

This is the slope.

We use the symbol \(b_1\) for the slope.

The formula is:

\[ b_1 = \frac{ \sum(X_i-\bar{X})(Y_i-\bar{Y}) }{ \sum(X_i-\bar{X})^2 } \]

This looks complicated, but each part is doing something fairly simple.

\[Xi−\bar{X}\]

Our average number of syllables is 2.25.

\[ 3-2.25=0.75 \]

\[ 1-2.25=-1.25 \]

4.4.3 What does 123.41 mean?

This is the important part.

Our predictor is number of syllables, and our outcome is duration in milliseconds.

Therefore:

For every additional syllable, the predicted duration increases by approximately 123.41 ms.

So \(b_1\) tells us about the relationship between X and Y.

In our data:

\[ b_1=123.41 \]

means:

+1 syllable \(\rightarrow\) approximately +123 ms

This is the slope of our regression line. We now know the steepness of the line, but we don’t yet know where to place the line vertically. Right now we can only say that every increase of 1 syllable corresponds to an increase of 123.41 ms.

4.5 Step 4: Calculate the intercept

We now know how steep our line should be.

But we still need to know where the line should be positioned.

This is the intercept, represented by \(b_0\).

We already know three things:

  • the mean number of syllables is 2.25;
  • the mean duration is 425.625 ms; and
  • the slope is 123.41 ms per syllable.

The regression line has to pass through the mean of X and the mean of Y:

\[ (2.25,425.625) \]

So we can work backwards to determine what the predicted duration would be when X is 0.

The formula is:

\[ b_0=\bar{Y}-b_1\bar{X} \]

Substituting our values:

\[ b_0=425.625-(123.41\times2.25) \]

\[ b_0=425.625-277.67 \]

\[ b_0=147.95 \]

So our intercept is approximately:

\[ b_0=148 \]

4.5.1 What does 148 mean?

The intercept is the predicted value of Y when X is 0.

Here:

  • X = number of syllables
  • Y = duration

So the intercept tells us:

The model predicts a duration of approximately 148 ms when the number of syllables is 0.

A zero-syllable word is not especially meaningful linguistically. That is okay.

The important point is that 148 is not an observation in our dataset. We did not measure a zero-syllable word lasting 148 ms.

It is a value calculated from the relationship among all eight observations. It tells us where our regression line crosses the Y-axis.

4.6 Step 5: Now we can write the regression equation

We have now calculated the two numbers that define our line:

\[ b_0=147.95 \]

and:

\[ b_1=123.41 \]

Only now do we need the general equation for a simple linear regression:

\[ \hat{Y}=b_0+b_1X \]

where:

  • \(X\) is the observed value of our predictor (number of syllables);
  • \(b_0\) is the intercept (duration is millisceonds at 0 length (the intercept));
  • \(b_1\) is the slope (change per syllable added (the slope)); and
  • \(\hat{Y}\) (“Y-hat”) is the value of Y predicted by the model (prediction of duration in ms).

For our particular dataset, therefore:

\[ \widehat{\text{Duration}}=147.95+123.41(\text{Syllables}) \]

Therefore, a word with one syllable is predicted to have a duration value of: 271 ms

\[ \widehat{271.36}=147.95+123.41(1) \] But cat actually lasted 280 ms. This gives us two different values:

\[ Y=280 \]

\[ \hat{Y}=271.36 \]

\(Y\) is what we observed.
\(\hat{Y}\) is what our model predicted.
They do not have to be identical. In fact, they normally will not be.

A word with two syllables is predicted to have a duration value of: 395 ms

\[ \widehat{394.778}=147.95+123.41(2) \]

A word with three syllables is predicted to have a duration value of: 518 ms

\[ \widehat{518.18}=147.95+123.41(3) \] A word with four syllables is predicted to have a duration value of: 642 ms. Note that we do not have any words with four syllables in our data. This is an example of the models power for predictablity.

\[ \widehat{641.59}=147.95+123.41(4) \]

The abstract symbols now correspond to actual things:

Symbol In our example Value/source
\(X\) Number of syllables Measured in the dataset
\(Y\) Actual word duration Measured in the dataset
\(b_0\) Intercept 147.95 ms
\(b_1\) Slope 123.41 ms per syllable
\(\hat{Y}\) Predicted duration Calculated by the model

A useful distinction is:

X and Y come from our observations. \(b_0\) and \(b_1\) are estimated from those observations. \(\hat{Y}\) is then calculated using the resulting model.

5.4 t-value

The t-value tells us how many standard errors the estimated coefficient is away from zero. The farther the t-value is from zero, the stronger the evidence that the coefficient is different from zero.

As a rough rule of thumb, a t-value of approximately ±1.96 or greater is often associated with statistical significance at the \(p < .05\) level in a two-tailed test. However, the exact critical value depends on the model’s degrees of freedom. With smaller samples, the required t-value is larger.

In our example, we have 6 degrees of freedom, so the critical value is approximately:

±2.447

I’m not going over the math, because, technically you should not be working with a dataset that has only 6 degrees of freedom. Ideally, to avoid deviating from the ±1.96 rule of thumb, you want a degrees of freedom value of approximately 500.

If you really want to verify the t-value critical limit, because you, for some reason, can not calculate a p-value, you can use:

5.5 P-values

The p-value tells us how surprising our result would be if the null hypothesis were true.

In our regression, the null hypothesis is that the predictor has no real effect:

\[ H_0:b_1=0 \]

A small p-value means that a result this extreme would be unlikely if the true slope were actually zero. The smaller the p-value, the stronger the evidence against the null hypothesis.

For example:

\[ p=0.04 \]

means that, if the null hypothesis were true, we would obtain a result at least this extreme about 4% of the time.

Conventionally, results with:

\[ p<0.05 \]

are described as statistically significant.

Importantly, a p-value is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true. Rather, it is the probability of obtaining a result this extreme, assuming that the null hypothesis is true.

5.5.1 How is the p-value calculated?

The p-value is calculated from the t-value and the model’s degrees of freedom.

Underneath this rather complex calculation is the Student’s t-distribution. For a two-tailed test, the p-value can be written as:

\[ p= 2\int_{|t|}^{\infty} \frac{ \Gamma\left(\frac{\nu+1}{2}\right) }{ \sqrt{\nu\pi}\Gamma\left(\frac{\nu}{2}\right) } \left( 1+\frac{x^2}{\nu} \right)^{-\frac{\nu+1}{2}} dx \]

where:

  • \(t\) is our observed t-value;
  • \(\nu\) is the degrees of freedom;
  • \(\Gamma\) is the Gamma function; and
  • the integral calculates the area in the tails of the t-distribution beyond our observed t-value.
If you want to see how this could be calculated in R, click here to reveal the code:

#Step 2: Calculate the Gamma functions

#Step 3: Calculate the constant at the front of the equation
constant <-
  gamma((df + 1) / 2) /
  (sqrt(df * pi) * gamma(df / 2))

constant

#Step 4: Define that t-distribution in R
t_distribution <- function(x) {
  
  constant *
    (1 + (x^2 / df))^(-(df + 1) / 2)
  
}

  t_distribution,

6 From one predictor to multiple regression

Regression models become particularly powerful for linguistic analysis because we can include multiple predictors in the same model, and those predictors can be of different types. For example, a regression might contain:

  • numeric predictors, such as age, speech rate, or number of syllables;
  • categorical predictors, such as sex, dialect, or word class;
  • ordinal predictors, such as proficiency level or generation.

This is important because linguistic variation is rarely determined by a single factor. The duration of a word, for example, might depend on the number of syllables it contains, characteristics of the speaker, the phonological environment, the dialect being spoken, and many other factors at the same time.

So far, our regression contained only one predictor:

\[ \hat{Y}=b_0+b_1X \]

For our word-duration example:

\[ \widehat{\text{Duration}} = b_0+b_1(\text{Syllables}) \]

We can extend exactly the same equation by adding another predictor:

\[ \hat{Y} = b_0+b_1X_1+b_2X_2 \]

For our data, let:

\[ X_1=\text{Syllables} \]

and:

\[ X_2=\text{Sex} \]

Because Sex is categorical, R needs to represent it numerically inside the model. With F as the reference category:

\[ F=0 \]

\[ M=1 \]

Our model is therefore:

\[ \widehat{\text{Duration}} = b_0+ b_1(\text{Syllables})+ b_2(\text{SexM}) \]

\[ b_0 = \text{intercept} \]

7.1 Multiple predictors

One of the major advantages of regression, as we saw in the previous section, is that we can include multiple predictors in the same model and estimate the contribution of each while accounting for the others.

This is especially important in linguistic analysis because linguistic variables are rarely influenced by only one factor. Word duration, for example, may depend simultaneously on the number of syllables, characteristics of the speaker, the speaker’s region, speech rate, phonological environment, and other linguistic or social factors.

A standard independent-samples t-test cannot accommodate this kind of structure because it compares two groups defined by a single predictor. Similarly, a standard chi-square test of independence examines the association between two categorical variables.

ANOVA can be extended further. A factorial ANOVA can contain multiple categorical predictors, while an ANCOVA (Analysis of Covariance) can combine categorical and numeric predictors. In fact, these belong to the same general linear-model framework as lm(). However, regression output gives us the individual coefficient estimates that are particularly useful for linguistic interpretation: the size and direction of an effect while the other predictors are held constant.

7.4 A note about multiple comparisons

Whenever we perform many related statistical tests, the chance of obtaining at least one statistically significant result simply by chance increases (a Type 1 error). This is called the multiple-comparisons problem.

For example, if we compare several groups using multiple t-tests, chi-square tests, or pairwise contrasts from a regression model, each test carries some chance of producing a false positive. If we keep testing enough comparisons at the usual \(p < 0.05\) threshold, the overall probability of finding at least one apparently significant result becomes larger than 5%.

One way to control this problem is the Bonferroni correction. The usual significance threshold is divided by the number of comparisons:

\[ \alpha_{\text{adjusted}} = \frac{\alpha}{\text{number of comparisons}} \]

For example, if we perform six related comparisons:

\[ \frac{0.05}{6} = 0.0083 \]

We would therefore require approximately \(p < 0.0083\) rather than \(p < 0.05\) for an individual comparison to be considered statistically significant.

Bonferroni is conservative, meaning that it reduces the chance of false positives but can also make genuine effects harder to detect. The important point is that multiple-comparison corrections are relevant whenever we conduct a family of related hypothesis tests, regardless of whether those tests come from t-tests, chi-square tests, ANOVA follow-ups, or regression contrasts.

For example, suppose we wanted to compare word duration between all four provinces in our dataset: AL, BC, ON, and SK.

With four groups, there are six possible pairwise comparisons:

  • AL vs. BC
  • AL vs. ON
  • AL vs. SK
  • BC vs. ON
  • BC vs. SK
  • ON vs. SK

We could run all six comparisons separately. However, if we treated every comparison as significant whenever \(p < 0.05\), we would increase our chance of making at least one Type I error.

In R, we could run the pairwise tests without any correction:

pairwise.t.test(
  word_data$Duration_ms,
  word_data$Prov,
  p.adjust.method = "none"
)

We also see that there’s a significant difference between ON vs. BC, and SK vs. BC when \(p<0.5\).
However, because there are multiple comparisons taking place, we would need to use Bonferroni correct.

To apply a Bonferroni correction, we instead use:

pairwise.t.test(
  word_data$Duration_ms,
  word_data$Prov,
  p.adjust.method = "bonferroni"

ON 1.000 0.232 -

P value adjustment method: bonferroni

After correction, we now see that none of the comparisons are significant at \(p<0.5\).

Because there are six comparisons, the Bonferroni-adjusted significance threshold is:

\[ \frac{0.05}{6}=0.0083 \]

R usually handles this by adjusting the p-values rather than displaying a new significance threshold. With Bonferroni, an uncorrected p-value is multiplied by the number of comparisons:

\[ p_{\text{adjusted}}=p\times6 \]

For example, the BC vs. AC comparison produced:

\[ p=0.028 \]

Without correcting for multiple comparisons, we would call this significant because \(p<.05\). After the Bonferroni correction:

\[ 0.028\times6=0.168 \] Note: there is a minor descripency between this result and the pairwise t-test output as we did not calculate the full range of decimals.

The adjusted result is therefore \(p=0.168\), which is not statistically significant.

By contrast, if the original result were \(p=.004\):

\[ .004\times6=.024 \]

it would remain statistically significant even after the correction.

But aren’t there already multiple comparisons in the regression output?

Yes. If Province has four levels and AL is the reference level, the regression output contains three coefficient tests: BC vs. AL, ON vs. AL, and SK vs. AL. These are three contrasts, although they are not all possible pairwise comparisons among the four provinces (there are six of those). Regression therefore does not automatically solve the multiple-comparisons problem. The important question is why those contrasts are being tested. If they were specified in advance because they correspond to particular research hypotheses, we do not necessarily apply a Bonferroni correction simply because several coefficients appear in the model. However, if we inspect many coefficients or rotate the reference level repeatedly in order to search for significant comparisons, we are conducting a family of exploratory tests and should consider a multiple-comparisons correction.

In other words: putting comparisons inside a regression model does not exempt them from multiple-comparison concerns; what matters is the family of hypotheses you are testing and whether those tests were planned or explored after seeing the data.

You might also encounter MANOVA (Multivariate Analysis of Variance). MANOVA is used when a study has more than one dependent variable and the researcher wants to test them jointly. Even if you don’t care about a specific speaker or word, mixed-effects models account for systematic variation among speakers and among words, producing more precise estimates and predictions within the fixed-effects.

At this point, the important idea is simply:

Ordinary regression assumes that every observation is independent. Mixed-effects regression allows us to account for observations that are grouped within speakers, words, items, or other repeated units.

We will examine how mixed-effects models do this in the next module.

Also, note. We are done with formal math equations, but this is what a mixed effects model would look like with 2 random effects and 4 fixed effects (a pretty common model structure):

\[ Y_{ijk} = \beta_0 +\beta_1X_{1ijk} +\beta_2X_{2ijk} +\beta_3X_{3ijk} +\beta_4X_{4ijk} +u_j +v_k +\epsilon_{ijk} \]

where:

\(\beta_0\) = overall intercept
\(\beta_1 \ldots \beta_4\) = coefficients for the four fixed effects
\(i\) = the individual observation/token
\(j\) = the speaker
\(k\) = the word
\(u_j\) = random intercept for grouping factor 1, e.g. Speaker
\(v_k\) = random intercept for grouping factor 2, e.g. Word
\(\epsilon_{ijk}\) = residual error

The random effects are assumed to vary around zero:

\[ u_j \sim N(0,\sigma^2_{\text{Speaker}}) \] \[ v_k \sim N(0,\sigma^2_{\text{Word}}) \]

9 Putting the Pieces Together

The progression from descriptive statistics to regression can be summarized as:

Describe the data
        ↓
Identify a relationship of interest
        ↓
Ask how much uncertainty surrounds the observed pattern
        ↓
Fit a regression model
        ↓
Estimate coefficients
        ↓
Compare estimates with their uncertainty
        ↓
Interpret the relationship in linguistic terms

For simple data, an ordinary regression may be sufficient.

For repeated linguistic observations, we will usually need to extend this structure using mixed effects.

10 Key Terms

Dependent variable (DV): The outcome the model attempts to explain.

Predictor / independent variable (IV): A variable used to explain variation in the dependent variable.

Intercept: The predicted value of the dependent variable when the predictor is zero, or when categorical predictors are at their reference level.

Slope / coefficient: The estimated change in the dependent variable associated with a one-unit change in a predictor.

Predicted value: The value estimated by the regression equation.

Residual: The difference between the observed value and the value predicted by the model.

Standard error: A measure of uncertainty around a coefficient estimate.

t-value: The coefficient estimate divided by its standard error.

p-value: The probability of obtaining the observed result, or something more extreme, under the null model.

Reference level: The categorical level against which other levels are compared.

Fixed effect: A predictor whose relationship with the dependent variable is directly estimated.

Random effect / grouping variable: A term that accounts for repeated or non-independent observations arising from speakers, words, items, or similar groups.

11 Summary

Regression is a way of describing how a dependent variable changes in relation to one or more predictors.

A simple regression fits a line:

\[ \hat{Y} = b_0 + b_1X \]

The intercept gives the baseline prediction, the slope gives the estimated effect of the predictor, and residuals measure the difference between observed and predicted values.

The same framework can represent familiar tests such as a two-group t-test, and it can be expanded to include multiple predictors.

The remaining problem is that linguistic datasets often contain repeated measurements from the same speakers and words. In the next module, we will extend the regression structure to account for this non-independence using linear mixed-effects models.
